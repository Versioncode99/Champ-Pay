The thesis begins at the merchant counter.

Nigeria’s cashless transition creates volume. The harder question is who turns that volume into better acceptance, settlement and treasury outcomes for the businesses underneath it.

The public case

Four propositions to test.

These are market arguments, not confidential company facts. Each can be examined against public infrastructure, merchant behaviour and competitive economics.

01 · Digital payment is normal

The adoption question has moved on. Reliability, cost and operational usefulness now decide which infrastructure earns durable merchant trust.

02 · Hardware changes the economics

A software-based acceptance surface can remove a distribution, maintenance and replacement burden from the model.

03 · The record creates leverage

Acceptance becomes more valuable when its data reconciles into the operating and treasury decisions that follow.

04 · In-market detail matters

Network quality, device mix, settlement habits, informal trade and institutional roles are product inputs—not localisation work for later.

The wedge

Start with the moment that repeats every day.

The merchant counter is where hardware cost, conversion, settlement timing and reconciliation become visible at once. Solving there creates a natural line of sight into the financial operations around the transaction.

That sequence is the strategic argument. Company-specific economics, execution evidence and forward plans are reserved for an authorised diligence process.

What to test

A diligence agenda, not a public data room.

The website is enough to decide whether the thesis deserves a conversation. The answers below require identity checks, context and confidentiality.
